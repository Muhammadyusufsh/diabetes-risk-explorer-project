Question & method

I analysed the UCI CDC Diabetes Health Indicators dataset with an 80/20 stratified train/test split. I compared an interpretable logistic-regression baseline with histogram gradient boosting. Results below come from 50,736 held-out rows; no test data was used to fit either model.

Why accuracy is not enough

Because only about 14% of rows are positive, a model that always says “no reported diabetes” would look accurate roughly 86% of the time while detecting nobody. I therefore report precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC, and average precision.

What the models show

The gradient-boosting model ranked outcomes more effectively (ROC-AUC 0.827; average precision 0.424), yet its recall at the default 0.50 threshold was only 15.8%. The logistic baseline found more of the positive class (76.1% recall) but at lower precision.

This is not a failure; it is the project’s main lesson: choosing a decision threshold is an ethical and context-dependent trade-off. A portfolio project can analyse it; it must not decide it for clinical use.

Honest findings & limitations

  • Average BMI was 27.81 in the no-reported-diabetes group and 31.94 in the prediabetes/diabetes group—an association, not a causal claim.
  • The target is self-reported survey status, not a clinical diagnosis.
  • The survey population, time period, feature definitions, and sampling process limit generalisation.
  • Demographic and socioeconomic fields may encode structural inequality. Any group analysis needs careful interpretation and must not support individual decisions.
